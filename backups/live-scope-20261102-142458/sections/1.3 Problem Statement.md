## 1.3 Problem Statement

The proposed eight-channel motor-imagery brain–computer interface (MI-BCI) must determine which decoded predictions can be accepted as commands for robotic-arm control and computer interaction. An incorrect prediction that reaches the command controller may produce an unintended action. Classification accuracy alone does not describe how reliably the system accepts commands, particularly when rejection is permitted. Rejecting too many predictions also reduces opportunities for interaction. The engineering problem therefore concerns controlling errors among accepted commands while retaining a useful command-acceptance rate.

This study considers three distinct conditions that may challenge command acceptance. Recording-session variability concerns differences between training recordings and a later session. Unfamiliar motor-imagery classes fall outside the decoder’s intended command vocabulary but may nevertheless receive a familiar label. Simulated channel degradation alters the EEG supplied to the decoder through defined disturbances or channel loss. These conditions require separate evaluation: identifying incorrect predictions among familiar classes does not establish the ability to reject unfamiliar imagery or tolerate degraded channels. The simulated conditions will represent specified signal disturbances, without establishing that the system can diagnose physical electrode faults.

Existing research provides relevant approaches. Song et al. (2022) investigated false-positive rejection in an asynchronous MI-BCI using a small number of EEG channels. Banville et al. (2022) investigated dynamic spatial filtering for learning from EEG affected by channel corruption. These studies support examining command rejection and signal robustness, but their findings do not establish the performance of the proposed eight-channel command pipeline across all three conditions. The question for this project is whether a lightweight reliability mechanism combining classification-confidence, feature-novelty and signal-quality indicators offers a favourable error–acceptance trade-off compared with established rejection approaches.

The primary research question is: **Can a computationally efficient, risk-aware selective-decoding framework reduce incorrect accepted motor-imagery commands under recording-session changes, unfamiliar motor-imagery classes and simulated channel degradation, compared with established rejection methods at equivalent command-acceptance rates?** Evaluation at equivalent acceptance rates will help distinguish improved error selection from simply rejecting more predictions. Reproducible experiments and a Raspberry Pi 4 demonstrator using prerecorded EEG replay will examine this question alongside computational requirements. A physical headset, live acquisition and physical robotic-arm integration remain conditional on resources and applicable safety and ethics approvals. Replay will demonstrate operation on the target computing platform; conclusions about live closed-loop reliability will require separate evidence.
