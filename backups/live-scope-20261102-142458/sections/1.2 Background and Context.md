## 1.2 Background and Context of the Project

A brain–computer interface converts measures of brain activity into information that can be used for communication or control. Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a non-invasive way to acquire such activity through electrodes on the scalp. An EEG channel records a potential difference between electrode sites or between a site and a reference. The recorded signal contains contributions from brain activity as well as other physiological and environmental sources. Its interpretation therefore depends on factors such as electrode placement, contact and recording conditions (Tangermann et al., 2012).
Motor imagery involves imagining a movement without performing it. During such tasks, changes in the strength of sensorimotor rhythms can be observed in EEG. A decrease in rhythmic power relative to a reference period is termed event-related desynchronization (ERD), while an increase is termed event-related synchronization (ERS). The patterns vary across people and tasks, but can provide information for distinguishing imagery classes, such as imagined left-hand and right-hand movement (Pfurtscheller et al., 2006).
A typical decoding process begins with EEG acquisition, followed by preprocessing to prepare the signals for analysis. The system then extracts signal features or learns representations from the data, and a classifier estimates which familiar motor-imagery class is present. A command layer can map the predicted class to an action. Each stage affects the information available to the next: for example, preprocessing choices and electrode locations influence the patterns presented to the decoder (Tangermann et al., 2012; Pfurtscheller et al., 2006).
An eight-channel arrangement limits the number of electrodes and acquisition channels that must be integrated into the proposed system. This can reduce setup and hardware complexity, while providing less spatial information than a larger montage. Channel placement consequently matters: signals useful for distinguishing motor-imagery tasks must be captured by the available electrodes. Eight channels are a project design choice, and their suitability depends on the task, placement and recording conditions (Pfurtscheller et al., 2006).
Embedded processing brings acquisition, decoding and command handling closer to the device being controlled. The proposed Raspberry Pi 4 provides a platform on which to deploy and measure the processing pipeline. In the required demonstrator, prerecorded EEG will be replayed through the system to evaluate its operation and computational demands. This evaluates processing on the target platform; it does not establish the behaviour of a live acquisition system.
For external interaction, accepted class predictions can be mapped to predefined robotic-arm movements or computer actions. A decoder with two imagery classes provides two class outputs, so a broader set of actions requires an additional selection strategy, such as modes or a state machine. A class estimate is therefore an input to an interaction controller; it does not by itself provide continuous or asynchronous control. The controller must also decide when an accepted command can be executed and how the application responds.
Reliability is relevant because a decoder can assign a familiar class to an input even when that input is difficult to interpret or belongs to an unfamiliar task. Recording sessions may differ, and a degraded channel can change the information supplied to the model. An incorrectly accepted command may cause an unintended action, while frequent rejection reduces opportunities for interaction. Selective decoding addresses this trade-off by allowing the system to withhold a prediction when its decision rule indicates insufficient evidence. The proposed project examines this balance alongside the engineering demands of an eight-channel BCI; the following section reviews relevant studies and the research gap.
