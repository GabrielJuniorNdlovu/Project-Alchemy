## 1.2 Background and Context of the Project

A non-invasive EEG brain–computer interface uses signals measured through scalp electrodes to support communication or control. Motor imagery involves imagining movement without performing the corresponding action. In an MI-BCI, a decoder uses patterns associated with instructed imagery tasks to estimate a command. Song et al. (2022) investigated an asynchronous MI-BCI with a small channel set and a mechanism for reducing false-positive detections. More recently, Ding et al. (2025) demonstrated online EEG-based robotic hand control using motor execution and motor imagery. These studies show that low-channel processing and robotic control have been investigated; neither establishes that the proposed eight-channel system will perform similarly.

EEG acquisition supplies measurements to a sequence of processing and decision stages. In the proposed system, scalp electrodes, a reference and a bias connection will connect to a compatible acquisition front end. Incoming samples will be timestamped, stored and prepared for decoding. The eight-channel configuration is a project constraint; electrode positions, sampling and referencing must be justified for the selected imagery tasks and available hardware. A custom wearable mount must support repeatable placement and adequate contact across recording occasions.

The project will begin with personally acquired recordings of familiar tasks, initially left-hand and right-hand imagery. Each trial will carry its instructed task label and timing information, while session identifiers and recording notes will preserve the circumstances of acquisition. The dataset will describe one participant across repeated sessions. It will therefore support investigation of that participant's model performance over time, with conclusions limited to the conditions observed. The number and duration of sessions remain research-design decisions. Published channel-reduction studies illustrate trade-offs among montage size, task classes and decoding performance (Meng et al., 2023; Özkahraman et al., 2025); their public-dataset results do not establish the suitability of any particular eight-channel montage for this study.

Changes between recording sessions are relevant to this design. Ma et al. (2022) described a dataset intended for cross-session MI research and reported different performance across within-session and cross-session conditions; its public recordings will not be used in this project's experiments. Huang et al. (2023) analysed within-person and between-person variability and found that the two settings differ in their signal-feature patterns. Other work has investigated transfer learning for session and subject shifts (George et al., 2022). Together, these studies support evaluating later sessions separately, but do not determine how this participant's recordings will behave. The planned personal-data partitions will distinguish model development, calibration and final testing. Revising a model after inspecting a test session changes the status of that session and must be documented.

The intended live processing path extends from acquisition through preprocessing, window construction and classification to a reliability decision. A class prediction estimates a familiar imagery label. The command gate decides whether that prediction can proceed, and an application controller maps an accepted prediction to an available action. These are separate decisions. Recent MI research has examined uncertainty estimates as a way to identify potentially incorrect classifications, while also reporting that more complex uncertainty methods do not necessarily outperform simple model outputs (Manivannan et al., 2024). This supports comparative evaluation rather than assuming a particular gate will be effective. Two imagery labels do not directly specify every robotic joint movement or computer command, so a finite-state interaction protocol will provide predefined navigation, selection and motion actions.

A further distinction concerns periods when no command is intended. A familiar-class classifier cannot be assumed to recognize rest merely because it produces confidence scores. Song et al. (2022) addressed false detections in asynchronous MI operation. Wu et al. (2024) likewise treated rest-versus-imagery screening as a separate stage from classifying the imagery task. The proposed demonstrator will initially restrict command opportunities through explicit arming and cued interaction; any later rest-detection function will require its own validation.

Raspberry Pi deployment will connect these decisions to the intended wearable system. Replay of personal recordings will support reproducible software checks before live input is integrated. The live evaluation must then establish what the complete acquisition-to-action system actually does.
